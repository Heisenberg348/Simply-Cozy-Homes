Our method

How We Test & Choose Products

Last reviewed: September 2026

Most “best of” lists for home products are assembled from other “best of” lists. Nobody opened the box. We can’t claim a laboratory, and we’re not going to pretend otherwise — so here is exactly what we do, in the order we do it, and what our recommendations are and aren’t worth.

Step one: start with the complaint, not the product

We don’t begin with a product category. We begin with a problem someone actually has: the cabinet under the sink where everything disappears behind the pipe, the rented bedroom with one dim ceiling socket, the linen closet that smells like the last time it rained. Framing it as a problem keeps us from recommending something clever that nobody needed.

Step two: read the one-star reviews first

Five-star reviews are written in week one, when everything is new and nothing has broken. One- and two-star reviews are written in month eight, and that’s where the useful information lives. We read them in bulk and look for the same complaint repeating — a hinge that fails, a coating that peels in the dishwasher, a “16 inch” shelf that measures 15 and a quarter.

A single furious review means one bad unit. Forty reviews describing the identical failure at the identical point means a design problem, and that disqualifies a product no matter how good the average score looks.

Step three: check whether the product is still the product

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it’s the one that protects you most. Popular home goods get quietly cheapened: steel becomes coated aluminum, solid wood becomes veneer, the manufacturer changes but the listing and its thousands of reviews stay put. So we sort reviews by most recent and compare them against the oldest ones. When the recent reviews sound like a different product, it is a different product, and the old ratings are worthless.

Step four: measure against a real space

We check dimensions against ordinary rooms and standard fittings rather than judging them in the abstract — a shelf unit against a typical closet depth, a lamp against a nightstand it has to share with a book and a glass of water. A storage bin that’s perfect for a walk-in pantry is the wrong answer for a studio apartment, and we say which is which instead of calling it “versatile.”

Step five: use it, when we can

Where a product is something we’d realistically own, we buy it with our own money and live with it. That means using it for weeks, not photographing it once. Hands-on time is stated plainly on the page: if we’ve used something for eight months we say eight months, and if we haven’t touched it we say the recommendation is research-based. You will never have to guess which kind you’re reading.

What we recommend, and how much of it

A finished guide names three things at most: the one we’d buy, a cheaper option that gives up something specific, and an upgrade that’s only worth it under a condition we spell out. If we can’t confidently name a winner, we publish the guide and say so. A list of twenty options isn’t research — it’s a decision handed back to you.

Four ways a product comes off the site

  • The design changed. New materials, new manufacturer, or a revision that reads worse in recent reviews.
  • A repeating fault appeared. The same failure showing up again and again after long-term use.
  • Something clearly better arrived. Better at the same price, or the same at a lower one.
  • It stopped being reliably available. Recommending a product that’s permanently out of stock wastes your time.

When a pick is pulled we replace it and note the change on the page. We don’t delete history quietly, because a recommendation you can’t audit isn’t worth much.

Why you’ll never see a price here

Prices on home goods move weekly, and a number typed into a web page is wrong within days. It’s also against the rules we operate under as an Amazon Associate to display a price that doesn’t update automatically. So we describe products by what they cost you in relative terms — the cheaper pick, the upgrade — and the live price stays where it belongs, on Amazon’s own listing.

How money enters the picture

We earn a commission when you buy through our links, at no extra cost to you. That commission is paid by Amazon out of their margin and it is identical whichever product you choose, so there is no version of this where steering you toward a worse product pays us better. No brand pays for a listing, a rating, or a kind sentence. Full detail is on our affiliate disclosure page.

What this method can’t do

We don’t run destructive lab tests, we don’t measure to laboratory tolerances, and we don’t test every product in every guide with our own hands. We’re a small editorial site, not a testing facility. What we offer instead is narrow focus, unusual patience with bad reviews, and a habit of checking whether a product is still the one people liked. If you need certified measurements, we’ll be the first to tell you to look elsewhere.
